## Quantifying Squaraine Phase Separation, Aggregate Populations, and Solar Cell Performance with a Correlated Multimethod Approach

Hamilton College — Published in the Journal of Physical Chemistry C

### Background & Motivation

Squaraine dyes are used as the light-absorbing donor material in a class of organic solar cells, where they are typically blended with an acceptor molecule to form the active layer. How well that blend performs depends heavily on its nanoscale morphology: squaraine molecules tend to aggregate and phase-separate from the acceptor during processing, and the resulting distribution of aggregate populations and phase domains has a direct effect on how efficiently the device converts light into current. This research set out to quantify that morphology directly and correlate it with measured solar cell performance, rather than relying on performance metrics alone to infer what was happening at the molecular scale.

### Methodology

I performed a long organic chemistry process to coat glass sheets with donor and acceptor molecules at varying blend ratios, then subjected the resulting films to different annealing conditions. Each of these solar cell samples was then imaged to determine film density and morphology, primarily using atomic force microscopy (AFM), alongside complementary optical and device measurements. Correlating results across this multimethod approach made it possible to connect molecular-scale phase separation and aggregate populations directly to measured solar cell performance across the different blend ratios and annealing conditions tested.

### Conclusion

This work demonstrated a correlated, multimethod approach to characterizing squaraine solar cell morphology, connecting blend ratio and annealing conditions to nanoscale phase separation, aggregate populations, and ultimately device performance. The research was conducted as part of my physics degree at Hamilton College and published in the Journal of Physical Chemistry C.
